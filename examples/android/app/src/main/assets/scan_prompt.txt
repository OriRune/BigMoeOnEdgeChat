The ferry to Karsholm left at six, which meant that anyone who wanted to be on the island by dark had to be standing on the quay by half past five, and Ines Varga was there at twenty past, her bag between her feet, watching the water slap against the stone as if it had something to say about the decision she had made.

She had not wanted to come. The letter from the solicitor had been three paragraphs long and said very little, except that her great-aunt Odile had died in March, that the house on the northern headland was hers, and that the keys could be collected from the harbourmaster, who would know where to find her. Ines had read it standing in the hallway of her flat, and then she had read it again sitting on the stairs, and then she had put it in a drawer and tried to forget it for five weeks.

What had changed her mind was a photograph. It had arrived in a plain envelope with no note, and it showed the house in winter: a square grey building with a slate roof, a single lit window on the upper floor, and behind it the sea, flat and pale as pewter. In front of the door stood a child of about seven in a yellow raincoat, looking straight into the camera with an expression of grave patience, as though she had been told to wait there and intended to do so for as long as necessary. The child was Ines. She knew it the way you know your own handwriting. She had no memory of the house, none at all, and she had never in her life been to Karsholm.

The ferry came in on time, which the old man beside her seemed to consider a personal insult. It was a small, blunt boat painted the green of old copper, and it smelled of diesel and wet rope. Ines took a seat by the window. The crossing took forty minutes, and for most of them there was nothing to see but fog, a moving wall of it, lit from inside by the last of the afternoon. Then, quite suddenly, the fog thinned and the island was there: low, dark, crowned with a ragged line of pines, and at its far end a pale tower that she took at first for a lighthouse and then understood was not.

The harbourmaster was a broad, unhurried woman named Brigid who handed over the keys without a word of condolence and looked at Ines for a moment longer than was comfortable. "You'll want the lamp," she said at last, pushing a brass storm lantern across the counter. "The generator hasn't run since March. And you'll not go up to the second floor tonight, whatever you hear."

"What would I hear?" Ines asked.

Brigid only shrugged and went back to her ledger. Outside, the light was going fast, and the path to the northern headland was a pale thread between the black shapes of the gorse. Ines lit the lantern, shouldered her bag, and began to walk.
